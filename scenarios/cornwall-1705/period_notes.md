# Cornwall 1705: Period notes

*Not secret: the player may read this. It holds world facts only, with no engineering advice. "≈" marks an approximate figure taken from scattered sources. Once the DM fixes a value inside a range in a ruling, that value is precedent. Prices are for west Cornwall in spring 1705, a cheap-grain year, in wartime. For convenience, and knowingly anachronistic: all wording is modern English, all measurements are metric, and money is in decimal pounds.*

## Money and units
- £1 = 100p. Game state keeps money in pence (`purse_p`): £50 = 5,000p.
- Coins: the gold guinea (≈£1.08; its value floats); the silver crown (25p), half-crown (12½p), shilling (5p), sixpence (2½p) and groat (≈1.7p); copper halfpennies and farthings (≈0.2p and 0.1p). Small change is scarce, so shops give credit and keep a tally. Mines pay monthly.
- Weights are in kg and tonnes, volumes in liters and m³, lengths in m, cm and mm. Coal is sold by the sack or the tonne, and timber by the cubic meter.
- Mine depths are measured down from the top of the shaft (the collar). Workmen's rules are marked to about 3 mm. There are no standard screw threads, gauges or scales: every nut is fitted to its own bolt.

## Prices (≈)
**Food and living.** Wheat ≈65–75p per 100 kg. A 1.8 kg wheat loaf costs ≈1.7–2.1p; barley bread, the usual Cornish loaf, is about a third cheaper. Beef or mutton 2.3–2.8p/kg, pork 2.8p, bacon 4.6–5.5p, butter 3.7–4.6p, cheese 1.8–2.8p. Eggs 7–10 for 1p. Salted pilchards (sardines) cost next to nothing in season (Jul–Nov): several for 0.1p. Salt 1.4–1.8p/kg (it is taxed). Small (weak) beer ≈0.2p a liter, ale ≈0.4–0.7p, cider ≈0.4p. Brandy with the duty paid is dear, and smuggled French brandy is everywhere. Sugar 5.5–7.3p/kg, tobacco 11–17p/kg, tea £1.30–£2.20/kg (gentry only).
- Inns: a set meal 2.5p; a good dinner 5p; a bed 1.7p shared, up to 5p private. A gentleman's room with meals in a Redruth house 25p–40p a week.
- Tallow candles 4.6–5.5p/kg (winter work burns a couple of kg a week). Soap 4.6p/kg. Paper 2.5p–5p for 24 sheets. A large-format book £1–£3.
- Clothes: workman's coat 50p–75p, shirt 15p, shoes 20p–25p, hat 10p–20p. Gentleman's suit £5–£10, wig £1–£5.
- Services: surgeon's visit 5p–25p; a Truro physician a guinea (≈£1.08). Attorney's consultation 33p; drawing up a written contract £1–£3. A one-sheet letter costs ≈1.3p over 130 km (≈0.8p within), usually paid by the person receiving it.

**Horses and fodder.** Pack-horse, mule or Cornish pony £2–£5; cart-horse £5–£8; good riding horse £10–£20; ox £5–£7. Hiring a saddle horse costs 5p–8p a day. Keeping a working horse costs ≈3.3–4.2p a day (hay £1.50–£2.50 a tonne, oats ≈40–55p per 100 kg); summer grazing is cheaper.

## Carriage and travel
- **Roads.** West of Truro most lanes take pack-horses and mules, not carts. Heavy loads go by ox cart or sledge over short hauls, and winter mud stops wheels altogether.
- **Pack-horse:** ≈100–125 kg per animal, ≈25–30 km a day, ≈4.6–6p per tonne-km. A wagon, where the road allows, costs ≈3p per tonne-km.
- **Sea freight.** Anything heavy or bulky comes by sea and is hauled the last few km. Coal freight from Swansea or Neath to Portreath costs ≈20p–30p a tonne; Bristol to the north Cornish coast ≈30p–50p a tonne; London to Falmouth ≈75p–£1.50 a tonne. Wartime adds 3–8% insurance, and waiting for convoys adds weeks.
- **Portreath** (Basset's Cove) has no quay, only a beach where small ships settle on the sand at low tide. No landing is possible in a northerly gale, so in winter there can be weeks without coal.

## Where materials come from
- **Iron.** None is smelted in Cornwall. Bar iron comes through Bristol. Cast goods come from the charcoal furnaces of the Forest of Dean and the Sussex and Kent Weald, where iron cannons are cast. No foundry west of Exeter casts anything bigger than pots.
- **Copper.** Cornish ore goes unsmelted by sea to smelters at Bristol and in South Wales (Neath, Swansea). Refined copper and brass come back at Bristol prices plus freight.
- **Brass** is made at Bristol from copper and Mendip calamine (zinc ore). The Baptist Mills company (1702) has Quaker partners, among them Abraham Darby, who brought in Dutch workmen in 1704. It sells hammered sheet and wire.
- **Tin** is local. It is smelted in charcoal-fired "blowing-houses" and in the newer coal-fired smelters, then taxed and stamped ("coined") at Truro, Helston or Penzance.
- **Lead** comes from Mendip, Wales and Derbyshire via Bristol. Plumbers cast it into sheet on beds of sand.
- **Coal.** Cornwall has none. It comes by sea from Wales (Swansea, Neath) and some from Bristol, and by the time it reaches a Redruth hearth it costs about twice the pithead price.
- **Timber.** The mining district is bare moor with small oak and ash in the valleys. Big timber comes from Devon, the Tamar, Plymouth and Bristol, and Norway or Baltic fir through the ports.
- **Leather and tallow** come from local cattle. **Hemp** is imported and made into rope at the port ropewalks.

## Institutions
**Stannaries and tin**
- Tin miners live partly under Stannary law, their own legal system. Stannary courts (the Vice-Warden's court) settle tin disputes, and the tinners' assembly (the Convocation, or "Stannary Parliament") rarely meets, at Truro. The Lord Warden since early 1705 is Francis Godolphin, Viscount Rialton, son of the Lord Treasurer.
- **Coinage (tin tax).** Tin is cast into blocks and taken to a coinage town (Truro, Helston, Penzance, Liskeard, Lostwithiel). There it is assayed (tested for purity), a corner (the "coign") is cut off, it is stamped, and a duty of ≈£4 a tonne is paid to the Duchy of Cornwall. Coinages are held at set seasons, chiefly Midsummer and Michaelmas (≈), and tinners are short of cash between them.
- **Pre-emption.** Since 1703 the Crown has bought up to ≈1,600 tonnes a year at ≈£69 a tonne, above the market price, under a seven-year contract. It steadies the tinners, but the money is slow to arrive.
- **Tin bounds.** Tinners may claim mining rights on unfenced common land by marking out its corners ("cutting bounds"), and the landowner takes a share ("toll"). Copper is not tin: copper is mined under ordinary leases.

**A mine and its accounts**
- A mine belongs to its adventurers (the mine's shareholders), who hold shares (e.g. sixteenths or thirty-seconds). The purser (treasurer) keeps the cost book (the mine's accounts). Every month or two a **count day** (shareholders' meeting, with dinner) either pays out profit as a dividend or levies a **call** (a demand for more money) on each share to cover losses.
- The lord of the soil (the landowner who owns the minerals) takes **dues** (a royalty) of ≈1/15 to 1/8 of the ore raised. The captain (mine manager) runs the underground work.
- On **setting day**, tributers (miners paid a share of the value of the ore they raise) bid for sections of the vein; tutworkers (miners paid for digging tunnels and shafts) are paid by the meter driven or sunk.
- **Local practice.** Mines have used gunpowder with fuses made from powder-filled quills since ≈1690. Adits (drainage tunnels) drain a mine down to valley level. Below the adit, water is raised by whims (horse-driven winches) working buckets or rag-and-chain pumps, and by water-wheels where a stream serves.

**Parish, church, law**
- Redruth parish has St Euny's church, west of the town at Churchtown. Gwennap parish (St Wenappa) lies to the east and includes St Day. Each parish has a vestry (parish council), churchwardens and overseers of the poor, collects the poor rate, and pays tithes. Under the settlement laws a parish can send a newcomer back to their home parish if they seem likely to need poor relief. A stranger with money is tolerated but watched.
- Justices of the Peace are local gentlemen. Quarter Sessions (the county court) are held locally and the Assizes (the higher court) at Launceston.
- The Church of England is the state church. Dissenters (Quakers, Presbyterians) have been tolerated since 1689. Catholics are distrusted, and in wartime strangers are suspected of spying for France. Queen Anne's Bounty for poor clergy was founded in 1704.

**Crown, Parliament, patents**
- Queen Anne reigns. The government is led by Lord Treasurer Sidney Godolphin, a Cornishman, and the Duke of Marlborough. Parliament was dissolved on 5 April 1705, with elections in May and June. Cornwall returns 44 members, mostly from small boroughs, and the gentry are busy campaigning and treating voters to food and drink.
- **Patents.** Letters patent under the Statute of Monopolies (1624) grant 14 years for "any manner of new manufacture", in England and Wales only (Scotland is a separate kingdom until 1707). The process runs from a petition to the Crown, through the Attorney-General's report, to the Signet, Privy Seal and Great Seal. It takes ≈2–4 months with a London agent and ≈£70–£120 in fees and tips. No written description is required yet. The holder enforces a patent himself by suing at Westminster, which is slow and costly.
- **Savery's patent.** Captain Thomas Savery's patent of 2 July 1698 covers "raising water and occasioning motion to all sorts of mill work by the impellent force of fire". An Act of 1699 extended it by 21 years beyond its 14, to 1733. His engine is described in his book *The Miner's Friend* (1702) and in a plate in Harris's *Lexicon Technicum* (1704), and he showed a model to the Royal Society on 14 June 1699. His engines serve houses and gardens near London. No mine is known to be drained by one, and miners doubt them.
- **Taxes.** Land tax is 20% of a property's assessed yearly value. There are excise taxes on beer, malt and salt, customs duties at the ports, and a tax on coal shipped by sea.

**The learned world**
- **The Royal Society** meets weekly at Gresham College, London. Its President is Isaac Newton, whom the Queen knights at Cambridge on 16 April 1705. The Secretary, Hans Sloane, edits the *Philosophical Transactions*, and Francis Hauksbee is curator of experiments (air-pump work). An outsider reaches the Society through a Fellow or by a letter read at a meeting. Its praise brings credit, not money.
- **Books** a gentleman can order through London or Bristol booksellers (allow weeks):
  - Moxon, *Mechanick Exercises* (smithing, joinery, turning)
  - Harris, *Lexicon Technicum* (1704)
  - Agricola, *De Re Metallica* (1556, in Latin; mine pumps and machines)
  - Boyle's works
  - Newton, *Opticks* (1704)
  - Savery, *The Miner's Friend*

## Calendar and time
- **Old Style.** England keeps the Julian calendar, 11 days behind the Continent: 2 April 1705 here is 13 April abroad. The legal year begins on Lady Day (25 March), so dates from 1 January to 24 March are written like "1705/6".
- **Quarter days** are Lady Day (25 Mar), Midsummer (24 Jun), Michaelmas (29 Sep) and Christmas (25 Dec). Rents, servants' wages, hirings and many accounts fall due on them.
- **1705 dates.** Monday 2 April falls in Holy Week: Good Friday is 6 April and Easter Sunday 8 April, and Easter Monday and Tuesday are holidays. Whitsunday is 27 May. Midsummer Eve (23 Jun) brings bonfires on the hills. Harvest runs Aug–Sep, and Michaelmas brings its goose.
- **Daylight at Redruth:** ≈13½ h in early April, 16½ h at midsummer, 8 h at midwinter. Local sun time runs ≈20 min behind London. Clocks are few, work runs from sunrise to sunset, and winter work burns candles.
- **Weather:** mild and wet, with ≈1,000–1,150 mm of rain a year, wettest Oct–Jan. Frost and snow are rare; gales hit the north coast Nov–Feb.

## Places and people
- **Redruth:** a market town (≈ a few hundred houses) strung along the Truro road and grown on mining. Its market is held twice weekly (Friday the main day, ≈). There are inns, the Fountain among them. St Euny's church is at Churchtown, and Carn Brea's granite hill and old castle rise to the south-west. The `smithy` is on the Truro road at the edge of town.
- **Gwennap and St Day** (east and south-east): old tin and copper workings, adits, and streamworks (open tin workings in stream beds) down the valleys toward Restronguet Creek and the Fal. Wheal Fortune lies in this ground.
- **Carnkie:** a mining hamlet under the south side of Carn Brea, home of Thomas Hocking the millwright.
- **Illogan and Tehidy** (north): seat of the Bassets, who own the mineral rights to much of the ground round Redruth and Camborne. The head of the family is Francis Basset (b. 1674), and they own Portreath. The Pendarves family of Pendarves lives near Camborne, to the west.
- **Truro:** coinage town, Stannary seat and port at the head of the Fal. It has the county's merchants, attorneys, smelting houses and best shops. The Boscawens of Tregothnan live nearby.
- **Falmouth and Penryn:** harbour and mail-ship station, with Pendennis Castle's garrison and the Killigrews of Arwenack. There are shipwrights, ropewalks and ship's chandlers, and press gangs (forced navy recruiters) in wartime. News from abroad lands here first.
- **Helston:** a coinage town. Godolphin House, seat of the Lord Treasurer's family, stands near Breage and its old mines.
- **Plymouth:** the naval dockyard at Plymouth Dock (1690s), with big timber, anchor smiths and shipwrights.
- **Speech:** English, with Cornish words ("bal" a mine, "wheal" a working, "knockers" the spirits underground). Some older people in the far west still speak Cornish. All speech is rendered here in modern English.
- People already known: Jacca Pascoe (smith, in your pay), Nicholas Penrose (purser, i.e. treasurer), Captain Richard Tregonning (mine captain, i.e. manager), Thomas Hocking (millwright). See `people/`.

## General knowledge of the day
- **Air has weight and "springiness".** Torricelli's tube (1643) holds ≈760 mm of mercury, and Pascal's mountain test followed in 1648. Boyle published his air-pump experiments in 1660 and his law of the air's springiness in 1662. Pump makers know as a fact of the trade that an ordinary pump cannot draw water up much more than ≈9 m. London instrument makers sell barometers ("weather-glasses").
- **Von Guericke** showed Magdeburg hemispheres that teams of horses could not pull apart (1654; described in his book of 1672). He also showed men unable to hold a piston back against an emptied cylinder.
- **Papin** made the "digester" (1679), a sealed pot with a weighted safety valve, shown to the Royal Society. In 1690 he proposed, in Latin in the *Acta Eruditorum*, a cylinder whose piston is moved by boiling and then cooling a little water in it. It has never been built at a useful size. He now lives in Hesse and corresponds with Leibniz.
- **Other engines of note:** Huygens' gunpowder cylinder (1673); the Marquis of Worcester's "water-commanding engine" (1663); Morland's plunger pump (1675); hand-pumped fire engines for London fires.
- **Heat.** Thermometers exist but no two agree, and nobody measures quantities of heat. **Mechanics** means Newton's laws and the simple machines (lever, wheel, pulley, screw). Engines are rated against horses and men.
- **Chemistry and medicine.** Chemistry still speaks of elements and "principles" (salt, sulphur, mercury), and assayers test ore in the furnace by rule of thumb. Medicine rests on the four humours, bleeding and purges. Smallpox is common and feared, and scurvy strikes on long voyages.
- **Popular belief:** knockers in the mines, charms, and dowsing for veins of ore with a hazel rod, which miners widely trust.
