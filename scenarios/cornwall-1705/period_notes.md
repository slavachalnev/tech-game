# Cornwall 1705: Period notes

*Not secret: the player may read this. It holds world facts only, with no engineering advice. "≈" marks an approximate figure taken from scattered sources. Once the DM fixes a value inside a range in a ruling, that value is precedent. Prices are for west Cornwall in spring 1705, a cheap-grain year, in wartime.*

## Money and units
- £1 = 20s; 1s = 12d; a farthing is ¼d. Game state keeps money in pence (`purse_d`): £50 = 12,000d.
- Coins: guinea (≈21s 6d; its value floats), crown 5s, half-crown, shilling, sixpence, groat 4d, and copper halfpence and farthings. Small change is scarce, so shops give credit on a tally. Mines pay monthly.
- Weight: cwt = 112 lb; ton = 20 cwt = 2,240 lb; stone = 14 lb. Coal is sold by the bushel (≈84 lb) or the ton, and timber by the load (50 ft³ squared).
- Gallons: the ale gallon is ≈282 in³ (≈10 lb of water) and the wine gallon 231 in³ (≈8⅓ lb). Miners mean the ale gallon.
- Length: fathom = 6 ft, used for all mine depths, measured down from the shaft collar. Rules are marked in inches and eighths. There are no standard screw threads, gauges or scales: every nut is fitted to its own bolt.

## Prices (≈)
**Food and living.** Wheat 28–32s a quarter. A 4-lb wheaten loaf costs 4–5d; barley bread, the common Cornish loaf, is about a third cheaper. Beef or mutton 2½–3d/lb, pork 3d, bacon 5–6d, butter 4–5d, cheese 2–3d. Eggs 3–4 a penny. Salt pilchards cost a farthing for several in season (Jul–Nov). Salt 1½–2d/lb (it is taxed). Small beer ½d a quart, ale 1–2d, cider 1d. Duty-paid brandy is dear, and smuggled French brandy is everywhere. Sugar 6–8d/lb, tobacco 1s–1s 6d/lb, tea 12s–20s/lb (gentry only).
- Inns: a set meal (an "ordinary") 6d; a good dinner 1s; a bed 4d shared, up to 1s private. A gentleman's room with board in a Redruth house 5s–8s a week.
- Tallow candles 5–6d/lb (winter work burns several pounds a week). Soap 5d/lb. Paper 6d–1s a quire. A folio book £1–£3.
- Clothes: workman's coat 10s–15s, shirt 3s, shoes 4s–5s, hat 2s–4s. Gentleman's suit £5–£10, wig £1–£5.
- Services: surgeon's visit 1s–5s; Truro physician a guinea. Attorney's consultation 6s 8d; drawing up articles of agreement £1–£3. A single-sheet letter costs 3d beyond 80 miles (2d within), usually paid by the receiver.

**Horses and fodder.** Pack-horse, mule or Cornish pony £2–£5; cart-horse £5–£8; good riding horse £10–£20; ox £5–£7. Hiring a saddle horse costs 1s–1s 6d a day. Keeping a working horse costs ≈8–10d a day (hay 30–50s a ton, oats 12–16s a quarter); summer grazing is cheaper.

## Carriage and travel
- **Roads.** West of Truro most lanes take pack-horses and mules, not carts. Heavy loads go by ox-wain or sledge over short hauls, and winter mud stops wheels altogether.
- **Pack-horse:** ≈2–2½ cwt per animal, 15–20 miles a day, ≈1s 6d–2s per ton-mile. A waggon, where the road allows, costs ≈1s per ton-mile.
- **Sea freight.** Anything heavy or bulky comes by sea and is hauled the last miles. Coal freight from Swansea or Neath to Portreath costs ≈4–6s a ton; Bristol to the north Cornish coast ≈6–10s a ton; London to Falmouth ≈15–30s a ton. Wartime adds 3–8% insurance, and waiting for convoys adds weeks.
- **Portreath** (Basset's Cove) has no quay, only a beach where small vessels ground at low tide. No landing is possible in a northerly gale, so in winter there can be weeks without coal.

## Where materials come from
- **Iron.** None is smelted in Cornwall. Bar iron comes through Bristol. Cast goods come from the charcoal furnaces of the Forest of Dean and the Sussex and Kent Weald, where iron guns are cast. No founder west of Exeter pours anything bigger than pots.
- **Copper.** Cornish ore goes raw by sea to smelters at Bristol and in South Wales (Neath, Swansea). Refined copper and brass come back at Bristol prices plus freight.
- **Brass** is made at Bristol from copper and Mendip calamine. The Baptist Mills company (1702) has Quaker partners, among them Abraham Darby, who brought in Dutch workmen in 1704. It sells sheet ("battery") and wire.
- **Tin** is local. It is smelted in charcoal blowing-houses and in the newer coal-fired houses, then coined at Truro, Helston or Penzance.
- **Lead** comes from Mendip, Wales and Derbyshire via Bristol. Plumbers cast it into sheet on sand beds.
- **Coal.** Cornwall has none. It comes by sea from Wales (Swansea, Neath) and some from Bristol, and it costs about twice the pithead price by the time it reaches a Redruth hearth.
- **Timber.** The mining district is bare moor with small oak and ash in the valleys. Big timber comes from Devon, the Tamar, Plymouth and Bristol, and Norway or Baltic fir through the ports.
- **Leather and tallow** come from local cattle. **Hemp** is imported and made into cordage at the port ropewalks.

## Institutions
**Stannaries and tin**
- Tinners live partly under Stannary law. Stannary courts (the Vice-Warden's court) settle tin disputes, and the Convocation, or "Stannary Parliament", sits rarely at Truro. The Lord Warden since early 1705 is Francis Godolphin, Viscount Rialton, son of the Lord Treasurer.
- **Coinage.** Tin is cast into blocks and taken to a coinage town (Truro, Helston, Penzance, Liskeard, Lostwithiel). There it is assayed, a corner (the "coign") is cut off, it is stamped, and 4s per cwt duty is paid to the Duchy. Coinages are held at set seasons, chiefly Midsummer and Michaelmas (≈), and tinners are short of cash between them.
- **Pre-emption.** Since 1703 the Crown has bought up to 1,600 tons a year at £3 10s per cwt, above the market price, under a seven-year contract. It steadies the tinners, but the money is slow to arrive.
- **Tin bounds.** Tinners may claim rights on unenclosed waste by cutting bounds, and the landowner takes a "toll" share. Copper is not tin: copper is mined under ordinary leases.

**A mine and its cost book**
- A mine belongs to its adventurers, who hold shares (e.g. sixteenths or thirty-seconds). The purser keeps the cost book. Every month or two a **count day** (an account meeting with dinner) either shares out profit as a dividend or levies a **call** on each share to cover losses.
- The lord of the soil takes **dues** of ≈1/15 to 1/8 of the ore raised. The captain runs the underground work.
- On **setting day**, tributers bid for pitches and take a share of the value of the ore they raise; tutworkers are paid by the fathom driven or sunk.
- **Local practice.** Mines have used gunpowder with quill fuses since ≈1690. Adits drain a mine down to valley level. Below the adit, water is raised by horse whims working buckets or rag-and-chain pumps, and by water-wheels where a stream serves.

**Parish, church, law**
- Redruth parish has St Euny's church, west of the town at Churchtown. Gwennap parish (St Wenappa) lies to the east and includes St Day. Each parish has a vestry, churchwardens and overseers of the poor, collects the poor rate, and pays tithes. Under the settlement laws a parish can remove a newcomer likely to need relief. A stranger with money is tolerated but watched.
- Justices of the Peace are local gentlemen. Quarter Sessions are held locally and the Assizes at Launceston.
- The Church of England is established. Dissenters (Quakers, Presbyterians) have been tolerated since 1689. Catholics are distrusted, and in wartime strangers are suspected of spying for France. Queen Anne's Bounty for poor clergy was founded in 1704.

**Crown, Parliament, patents**
- Queen Anne reigns. The ministry is led by Lord Treasurer Sidney Godolphin, a Cornishman, and the Duke of Marlborough. Parliament was dissolved on 5 April 1705, with elections in May and June. Cornwall returns 44 members, mostly from small boroughs, and the gentry are busy canvassing and treating.
- **Patents.** Letters patent under the Statute of Monopolies (1624) grant 14 years for "any manner of new manufacture", in England and Wales only (Scotland is a separate kingdom until 1707). The path runs from a petition to the Crown, through the Attorney-General's report, to the Signet, Privy Seal and Great Seal. It takes ≈2–4 months with a London agent and ≈£70–£120 in fees and gratuities. No written description is required yet. The holder enforces a patent himself by suing at Westminster, which is slow and costly.
- **Savery's patent.** Captain Thomas Savery's patent of 2 July 1698 covers "raising water and occasioning motion to all sorts of mill work by the impellent force of fire". An Act of 1699 extended it by 21 years beyond its 14, to 1733. His engine is described in his book *The Miner's Friend* (1702) and in a plate in Harris's *Lexicon Technicum* (1704), and he showed a model to the Royal Society on 14 June 1699. His engines serve houses and gardens near London. No mine is known to be drained by one, and miners doubt them.
- **Taxes.** Land tax is 4s in the £. There is excise on beer, malt and salt, customs at the ports, and a duty on coal carried by sea.

**The learned world**
- **The Royal Society** meets weekly at Gresham College, London. Its President is Isaac Newton, whom the Queen knights at Cambridge on 16 April 1705. The Secretary, Hans Sloane, edits the *Philosophical Transactions*, and Francis Hauksbee is curator of experiments (air-pump work). An outsider reaches the Society through a Fellow or by a letter read at a meeting. Its praise brings credit, not money.
- **Books** a gentleman can order through London or Bristol booksellers (allow weeks):
  - Moxon, *Mechanick Exercises* (smithing, joinery, turning)
  - Harris, *Lexicon Technicum* (1704)
  - Agricola, *De Re Metallica* (1556, in Latin; mine pumps and machines)
  - Boyle's works
  - Newton, *Opticks* (1704)
  - Savery, *The Miner's Friend*

## Calendar and time
- **Old Style.** England keeps the Julian calendar, 11 days behind the Continent: 2 April 1705 here is 13 April abroad. The legal year begins on Lady Day (25 March), so dates from 1 January to 24 March are written like "1705/6".
- **Quarter days** are Lady Day (25 Mar), Midsummer (24 Jun), Michaelmas (29 Sep) and Christmas (25 Dec). They fall due for rents, servants' wages, hirings and many accounts.
- **1705 dates.** Monday 2 April falls in Holy Week: Good Friday is 6 April and Easter Sunday 8 April, and Easter Monday and Tuesday are idle days. Whitsunday is 27 May. Midsummer Eve (23 Jun) brings bonfires on the hills. Harvest runs Aug–Sep, and Michaelmas brings its goose.
- **Daylight at Redruth:** ≈13½ h in early April, 16½ h at midsummer, 8 h at midwinter. Local sun time runs ≈20 min behind London. Clocks are few, work runs from sun to sun, and winter work burns candles.
- **Weather:** mild and wet, with ≈40–45 in of rain a year, wettest Oct–Jan. Frost and snow are rare; gales hit the north coast Nov–Feb.

## Places and people
- **Redruth:** a market town (≈ a few hundred houses) strung along the Truro road and grown on mining. Its market is held twice weekly (Friday the main day, ≈). There are inns, the Fountain among them. St Euny's church is at Churchtown, and Carn Brea's granite hill and old castle rise to the south-west. The `smithy` is on the Truro road at the edge of town.
- **Gwennap and St Day** (east and south-east): old tin and copper workings, adits, and streamworks down the valleys toward Restronguet Creek and the Fal. Wheal Fortune lies in this ground.
- **Carnkie:** a mining hamlet under the south side of Carn Brea, home of Thomas Hocking the millwright.
- **Illogan and Tehidy** (north): seat of the Bassets, lords of much of the mineral ground round Redruth and Camborne. The head of the family is Francis Basset (b. 1674), and they own Portreath. The Pendarves family of Pendarves lives near Camborne, to the west.
- **Truro:** coinage town, Stannary seat and port at the head of the Fal. It has the county's merchants, attorneys, smelting houses and best shops. The Boscawens of Tregothnan live nearby.
- **Falmouth and Penryn:** harbour and packet station, with Pendennis Castle's garrison and the Killigrews of Arwenack. There are shipwrights, ropewalks and chandlers, and press gangs in wartime. News from abroad lands here first.
- **Helston:** a coinage town. Godolphin House, seat of the Lord Treasurer's family, stands near Breage and its old mines.
- **Plymouth:** the naval dockyard at Plymouth Dock (1690s), with big timber, anchor smiths and shipwrights.
- **Speech:** English, with Cornish words ("bal" a mine, "wheal" a working, "knockers" the spirits underground). Some older folk in the far west still speak Cornish.
- People already known: Jacca Pascoe (smith, in your pay), Nicholas Penrose (purser), Captain Richard Tregonning (mine captain), Thomas Hocking (millwright). See `people/`.

## General knowledge of the day
- **Air has weight and a "spring".** Torricelli's tube (1643) holds ≈30 in of mercury, and Pascal's mountain test followed in 1648. Boyle published his air-pump experiments in 1660 and the law of the air's spring in 1662. Pump makers know as a fact of the trade that a common pump cannot draw water much above ≈30 ft. London instrument makers sell barometers ("weather-glasses").
- **Von Guericke** showed Magdeburg hemispheres that teams of horses could not pull apart (1654; described in his book of 1672). He also showed men unable to hold a piston back against an emptied cylinder.
- **Papin** made the "digester" (1679), a sealed pot with a weighted safety valve, shown to the Royal Society. In 1690 he proposed, in Latin in the *Acta Eruditorum*, a cylinder whose piston is moved by boiling and then cooling a little water in it. It has never been built at a useful size. He now lives in Hesse and corresponds with Leibniz.
- **Other engines of note:** Huygens' gunpowder cylinder (1673); the Marquis of Worcester's "water-commanding engine" (1663); Morland's plunger pump (1675); hand-pumped fire engines for London fires.
- **Heat.** Thermometers exist but no two agree, and nobody measures quantities of heat. **Mechanics** means Newton's laws and the "mechanical powers" (lever, wheel, pulley, screw). Engines are rated against horses and men.
- **Chemistry and medicine.** Chemistry still speaks of elements and "principles" (salt, sulphur, mercury), and assayers work fire assays by practice. Medicine rests on the humours, bleeding and purges. Smallpox is common and feared, and scurvy strikes on long voyages.
- **Popular belief:** knockers in the mines, charms, and dowsing for lodes with a hazel rod, which miners widely trust.
